Synthetic Reading: A Human–AI Comparative Analysis of The Party by Sayantani Dasgupta

Author of the literary text: Sayantani Dasgupta  ·  Researcher: Sangita Chatterjee, MA Digital Humanities, IIT (ISM) Dhanbad  ·  Field: Digital Humanities

Research question. How does Sayantani Dasgupta's The Party represent the intersections of gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability, and how can synthetic reading reveal these socio-cultural structures through human–AI comparative literary analysis?

Abstract

This project reads one short story, a second-person account of an evening at a former colleague's home in Delhi, in two ways. The researcher reads the opening of each of four blocks (the partial reading), records preliminary themes for each block and writes a full-text interpretation. An AI reader then extracts themes from the full text under a strict prompt that demands exact quotations and separates explicit evidence from inference. Every quotation attached to a theme (93) is machine-checked against the corpus. Five visualisations compare the human and AI readings and map how gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability share evidence. The aim is to see what each reader notices, not to replace interpretation. Step 7 interprets these patterns through theories of performativity, compulsory heterosexuality, the closet, cultural capital and affect, and argues that the story binds its four dimensions into one economy of appetite and display.

Objectives

  1. Document the partial reading of four blocks from the highlighted passages.
  2. Compare the researcher's preliminary themes and full-text interpretation with a full-text AI extraction.
  3. Build a clean corpus, a reusable prompt and a validated structured dataset.
  4. Visualise distribution, co-occurrence, agreement and intersections without treating word counts as proof.
  5. Interpret the patterns through selected theories and answer the research question.

Step 2 · Research methodology and reflexivity

Research problem

How do a human and an AI reader identify and weigh the story's intertwined class, gender, sexuality and emotional structures?

Objectives

Documented partial reading; evidence-bound extraction; transparent comparison; visual mapping of intersections.

Research questions

(1) How does the story represent the four dimensions? (2) Where do human and AI readings agree or diverge? (3) What do the shared-evidence patterns suggest for Step 7?

Research design and approach

Qualitative and interpretive, close to reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Computation organises interpretations (counts of verified quotations, shared-paragraph co-occurrence, agreement tables), in the spirit of algorithmic criticism (Ramsay, 2011). Word frequency is not used as evidence of a theme.

Theoretical frameworks, used selectively

FrameworkWhy it is relevant to this story
Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989, 1991)Salary, gender and sexuality shape the narrator's position together; class envy and bodily self-surveillance are linked.
Gender performativity (Butler, 1990)Mahesh's staged authority, Tanu's devotion and the narrator's body-checking read as repeated, regulated performances.
Heteronormativity and queer theory (Rich, 1980; Sedgwick, 1990; Warner, 1991)Desire for a woman is imaginable only "if you were a man"; Mahesh cannot accept his wife's past.
Cultural capital (Bourdieu, 1984, 1986)Intellectual talk, furnishing and hospitality work as display.
Affect theory (Ahmed, 2004)Shame, envy and disgust circulate between characters and stick to objects (food, bodies, the mother's remark).
Feminist and queer literary criticismReading practices for who speaks, whose labour is invisible and what is closeted.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The researcher reads a story set in contemporary New Delhi from within the Indian academic and social context it depicts. That helps with details (a dhaba, Hindi films, expectations around marriage and pregnancy) but may also carry assumptions about class, marriage and sexuality. Her preliminary themes were written before the AI extraction and are kept unchanged so that the comparison is honest.

def clean_text(s):
    s = re.sub(r'[\u00a0\u2009\u200a\u202f\u2002\u2003]', ' ', s)   # hair/thin/non-breaking spaces from the source -> normal space
    for k, v in SPACED_ABBR.items():
        s = s.replace(k, v)
    s = re.sub(r'\s+([!?.,;:])', r'\1', s)      # "sucker !" -> "sucker!"
    s = re.sub(r'[ \t]+', ' ', s).strip()
    return s

def split_sentences(par):
    """Rule-based segmentation that keeps quotation marks with their sentence."""
    parts = re.split(r'(?<=[.!?])["”’]?\s+(?=["“‘]?[A-Z])', par)
    return [p.strip() for p in parts if p.strip()]

def tokenize(s):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z]+(?:['’][a-z]+)?", s.lower())

Step 4 · AI prompt engineering

Feature-extraction framework

The prompt asks for 14 kinds of features per block (themes and subthemes, gender, sexuality and identity, class, patriarchal power, affect, relationships, conflicts, social conditioning, internalised prejudice, symbolism, narrative perspective, character development, textual evidence) and tags each theme with the four dimensions.

Rules that control the extraction

  • Every theme needs verbatim evidence (quotation ≤ 25 words and a paragraph ID).
  • Each evidence item is labelled explicit or inferential.
  • Each classification is explained; overlapping themes are listed; ambiguity is kept in an ambiguities field.
  • No invented characters, quotations or events; the narrator stays unnamed.
  • Output is one JSON object.

Block 1: Social class, alienation and appearances

Main focus: Class, economic insecurity, social status, workplace relationships, insecurity and alienation.

The narrator enters the party already carrying a strong awareness of her economic position. She compares her own rented life with Tanu and Mahesh's expensive house and lifestyle. She is happy for Tanu, but her happiness is mixed with envy because she knows that people earning approximately the same salary do not necessarily have the same standard of living.

From a human perspective, this shows that class is experienced emotionally, not simply through income. The narrator feels that she does not completely belong in this social environment. Even though these are former colleagues and familiar people, she feels like an outsider.

Her insecurity also becomes visible through her interaction with Mahesh. When he compliments her black shirt, she immediately thinks about her body and wonders whether he would have looked at her differently if she were less flat-chested.

Therefore, class and appearance intersect. Her sense of being economically less privileged is accompanied by a feeling that she is also somehow less attractive, less confident and less socially successful.

Human interpretation: The party becomes a space where the narrator silently compares herself with others. She does not openly complain about inequality, but she constantly measures herself against people who appear more successful, attractive, confident and socially secure.

Connection to research question: This block demonstrates that class is connected with emotional vulnerability. Social inequality produces not only material differences but also feelings of inadequacy, loneliness and exclusion.

Coded themes: Class consciousness and economic insecurity Alienation and social exclusion Gendered body and self-consciousness

Block 2: Sexuality, desire and identity

Main focus: Sexual identity, repressed desire, same-sex relationships, heteronormativity, internalised prejudice and sexual anxiety.

This is one of the most psychologically complicated parts of the story.

The narrator observes Pia and Projapoti's relationship and feels uncomfortable with their physical affection. She describes their public intimacy negatively, yet she also remembers imagining Tanu and Projapoti together sexually. She immediately tries to reassure herself that these thoughts do not mean that she is a lesbian.

A human reading reveals an important contradiction here:

She is simultaneously attracted to, curious about and uncomfortable with female sexuality.

She has been socially conditioned to understand desire primarily through heterosexuality. Therefore, when she experiences same-sex-related thoughts, she does not explore them openly. Instead, she explains them away.

The sentence where she imagines Tanu but then says that she is not a man is particularly significant. Her reasoning suggests that she has difficulty accepting the possibility that a woman could experience desire toward another woman.

Human interpretation: The narrator's sexuality is not presented as a clear identity. Instead, it appears as an internal conflict between curiosity, attraction, social expectations and self-denial.

At the same time, she watches Rani's attraction toward Mahesh and becomes highly conscious of sexual desire around her. Rani's body language, Tanu's reaction to Rani, and Mahesh's position as the object of attention make the narrator increasingly aware of sexuality and her own lack of sexual experience.

Connection to research question: This block shows how sexuality intersects with emotional vulnerability. The narrator's uncertainty about sexuality is connected to loneliness, lack of sexual experience, jealousy and her desire to be desired.

Coded themes: Repressed desire and sexual identity Heteronormativity and internalised prejudice Desire, jealousy and triangulation Sexual anxiety Alienation and social exclusion

Block 3: Gender roles, power and emotional vulnerability

Main focus: Gender stereotypes, male dominance, emotional dependency, patriarchal relationships, people-pleasing, childhood trauma and self-worth.

This block provides perhaps the strongest explanation of why the narrator behaves the way she does.

Mahesh tells Tanu that she does not need to help in the kitchen because she deserves a complete break from it. On the surface, this appears caring and affectionate. Tanu responds with visible happiness. Meanwhile, the narrator is immediately assigned work in the kitchen—cutting onions and garlic.

The interesting human interpretation is that care and power exist simultaneously.

Mahesh appears loving toward Tanu, but he also controls who does what. Tanu's happiness is connected to his approval and affection, while the narrator automatically accepts the subordinate role of helping.

The childhood sandwich episode makes this behaviour much deeper. As a child, the narrator was proud that she accepted the unattractive sandwich rather than rejecting it. But her mother privately described her as a "spineless child."

This memory explains her adult behaviour.

She has learned to believe that:

being accommodating = being weak

Yet she continues to accommodate everyone.

She spends almost two hours helping Mahesh in the kitchen even though she is hungry, tired and uncomfortable. She repeatedly wants to leave but stays because she does not want to appear rude.

Human interpretation: Her "people-pleasing" is not simply a personality trait. It is connected to childhood conditioning and her fear of being judged as inadequate.

The narrator wants to become assertive, but she does not know what healthy assertiveness looks like. This becomes important later when she finally speaks out.

Connection to research question: Gender, emotional vulnerability and power are closely connected here. The story suggests that social expectations about how women should behave—being polite, accommodating and emotionally controlled—can shape their sense of self-worth.

Coded themes: Male authority and patriarchal performance Devotion and emotional dependency People-pleasing and social conditioning Maternal judgement, childhood shame and self-worth Hunger, hospitality and unequal labour

Block 4: Truth, betrayal and self-assertion

Main focus: Emotional manipulation, jealousy, hidden relationships, disclosure, betrayal, moral conflict and the power of speech.

This is the culmination of the previous three blocks.

Auro asks the narrator what she is doing afterwards. She is simultaneously "fearful and desperate" because she wants the opportunity but is frightened by what it might mean.

Then Mahesh tells her that she must stay.

She automatically agrees.

This moment is extremely important because she immediately connects her behaviour with her mother's childhood accusation:

"No, Ma, I was not spineless."

Her past and present collide.

Later, Auro leaves with Rani, and the narrator interprets his behaviour as another rejection. Her frustration becomes connected to her virginity, her desire and her sense of inadequacy. She eventually decides that she is done being the passive child she once was.

But her attempt at self-assertion is morally complicated.

She exposes Tanu's previous relationship with Projapoti by asking:

"Tanu, does it feel weird watching Projapoti with another woman?"

This immediately changes the emotional atmosphere of the room.

Human interpretation: The narrator finally uses her voice, but she uses it as a weapon.

This is why the ending should not simply be interpreted as "the woman becomes empowered." Her assertion comes from a mixture of:

  • anger,
  • jealousy,
  • sexual frustration,
  • humiliation,
  • insecurity,
  • resentment,
  • and the desire to prove that she is no longer "spineless."

She recognizes Mahesh's vulnerability just before making the statement. She understands that he is deeply afraid of losing Tanu, yet she still chooses to reveal something that can hurt him and Tanu.

So her final act is both empowerment and betrayal.

Connection to research question: This block brings together gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability. Her social insecurity and sexual frustration influence the way she uses truth. Sexual identity becomes a source of conflict, while gendered expectations about assertiveness shape her final decision.

Coded themes: Male authority and patriarchal performance People-pleasing and social conditioning Maternal judgement, childhood shame and self-worth Desire, jealousy and triangulation Sexual anxiety Repressed desire and sexual identity Truth, disclosure and betrayal Self-assertion, spite and moral ambiguity Male vulnerability and insecurity

Overall human interpretation

The four blocks can be understood as a psychological progression:

  • Block 1 → I feel inferior and outside the group.
  • Block 2 → I become increasingly aware of sexuality and my own desire.
  • Block 3 → I understand that my passivity comes from gender expectations and childhood conditioning.
  • Block 4 → I finally use my voice, but my assertion becomes an act of emotional violence.

Therefore, the story does not present gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability as four separate themes. They constantly influence one another.

The intersection is the key finding

  • Class → makes the narrator feel socially inferior.
  • Gender → teaches her to be accommodating and non-confrontational.
  • Sexuality → creates desire, confusion, jealousy and insecurity.
  • Emotional vulnerability → causes her to internalise all these pressures.
  • Self-assertion → eventually emerges, but in a destructive form.

This makes the narrator a complex human subject rather than simply a victim or an empowered woman. Her final action is neither completely right nor completely wrong. It emerges from accumulated experiences throughout the evening.

In one research-oriented sentence

Human interpretation suggests that The Party portrays gender, sexuality and class not as independent social categories, but as intersecting forces that shape the narrator's insecurity, desire, relationships and eventual use of speech as a form of both agency and retaliation.

7.1 The argument in brief

The Party represents gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability as one economy of appetite and display rather than four separate topics. Class is first felt as hunger and as envy of a door. Through the gendered work of hospitality it becomes a question of who cooks, who serves and who is listened to. Heterosexual marriage is the house that secures Tanu's class position, and the closet is its price. The narrator is single, poorer, sexually inexperienced and shamed since childhood. She can describe her own desire only in heteronormative terms, and when she finally asserts herself she does it by opening someone else's closet. The story's one act of “spine” is an act of exposure.

7.2 Interpretation by dimension

Class: hunger, display and the door

Starts from Fig. 1 (class evidenced in Blocks 1–2 only), Fig. 3 (class unlinked to any other theme), Fig. 5 (class the smallest dimension, 22 items)   Lens Bourdieu, cultural capital

The narrator measures herself first in money. She lives in the “hinterlands” because “that’s all you can afford on your salary” B1P2, and at the hosts' door she wonders how many people of her age and pay get to “disappear behind a door such as this” B1P4. The drawing room completes the picture: stainless steel and white leather, coffee-table books lit “just so”, and an argument about Nietzsche. In Bourdieu's terms this is cultural capital on display: taste and intellectual talk working as signs of position (Bourdieu, 1984, 1986).

Step 6 shows class disappearing after Block 2. That result should not be read literally. What disappears is the explicit vocabulary of salary; class itself changes register. It becomes hunger (two bowls of potato chips presented as “appetizers”), unpaid labour (two hours in a hot kitchen) and taste (a biryani that is overcooked, undercooked and over-salted at once). The theme that carries it forward is hunger, hospitality and unequal labour (T16, 9 items, tagged class and gender), which runs through Blocks 2–4. The hosts have the house and the furnishings but not the substance of hospitality: no ice, no food for hours, a meal that fails. Display and care have come apart.

The expensive door also anticipates the end of the story. What lies behind it is a marriage whose stability depends on something not being said. The class security the narrator envies and the closet she will later open belong to the same house.

Gender: authority as performance, hospitality as labour

Starts from Fig. 2 (male authority has the most evidence, 12 items), Fig. 3 (male authority and hunger/hospitality/labour share paragraphs)   Lens Butler, gender performativity

Mahesh's authority is staged rather than enforced. He sighs “dramatically”, smacks his knee “theatrically”, and the light on his shaved head gives him the look of “an enlightened guru dispensing wisdom to his disciples” B2P15. Butler's argument that gender is produced through repeated performances before an audience (Butler, 1990) fits closely, because his masculinity needs spectators: Tanu's eyes are filled “more with the ardor of a devotee than the love of a wife” B2P2, and Rani gazes at him as at a work of art.

Hospitality is where the performance turns into a politics of labour. Mahesh releases his wife from the kitchen as a gift (“Tonight, you get full release from the kitchen.” B3P7), but the work is not abolished; it is moved to another woman, the guest who is “too well brought up to not offer help” B3P4. In the kitchen her job is to chop and to listen: “It was him listening to himself spread wisdom” B3P11. The shared evidence between male authority and hospitality in Fig. 3 therefore points to a structure, not a coincidence: a benevolent patriarchy that takes credit for care whose cost falls on women. His protectiveness is the same possessiveness in another form, from the arm “possessively gripped” around Tanu's waist to his wedding-night list of “what he would like to do to every man who has ever hurt Tanu” B1P8.

Sexuality: compulsory heterosexuality and the open secret

Starts from Fig. 2 (heteronormativity and desire/jealousy, 9 items each), Fig. 4 (repressed desire marked by the human reader in Block 4)   Lens Rich; Sedgwick; Warner; Ahmed on disgust

The narrator's fantasy about Tanu is policed in the same breath that describes it. “If you were a man” B2P13, she could have liked Tanu's hair and praised her smile; since she is not, the thoughts are “silly and pointless”, and she adds: “It certainly didn’t mean you were a lesbian.” B2P13 Rich's account of compulsory heterosexuality (1980) describes this exactly: desire for a woman becomes thinkable only by imagining oneself into the male position. The narrator's sexuality is never declared, and the reading should not declare it for her. What the text shows is a desire that can be held only in heteronormative grammar.

Her reaction to Pia and Projapoti, when “your stomach churns” B2P12, can be read through Ahmed's account of disgust as an emotion that draws a boundary between the self and what it fears becoming (Ahmed, 2004). The text leaves open whether the feeling is prejudice, envy or desire, and the extraction rightly keeps this as an ambiguity.

Tanu's past with Projapoti works as what Sedgwick calls the epistemology of the closet (Sedgwick, 1990): the office knows, the husband does not, and that unequal distribution of knowledge is a form of power that anyone in the know can use. The narrator reads Mahesh's ignorance as structural, not accidental: he “cannot swallow the idea of his wife ever having been satisfied by a woman” B4P24. Heteronormative marriage (Warner, 1991) here rests on an enforced forgetting, and the couple's plan (“We’re trying to get pregnant.” B4P11) is the project that would seal it.

Emotional vulnerability: shame that moves

Starts from Fig. 1 (truth/disclosure and self-assertion appear only in Block 4), Fig. 4 (male vulnerability: absent from the key words, present in the full-text human interpretation and the AI extraction), Fig. 5 (largest overlap: gender and emotional vulnerability, 19 items)   Lens Ahmed, the cultural politics of emotion

Ahmed argues that emotions are not private states but move between bodies and stick to objects (Ahmed, 2004). The narrator's shame has a precise origin, her mother's “I cannot believe I gave birth to a spineless child.” B3P10, and a precise object, the collapsed sandwich she chose to love. The story then shows the shame moving. When Mahesh insists that she stay, she obeys and answers her mother silently: “No, Ma, I was not spineless.” B4P6 The sentence fails as it is spoken, because she has just given way again.

The disclosure comes at the moment she sees Mahesh as “a man frightened that he might lose what he loves most in his life” B4P21. She recognises vulnerability and strikes at it. Her question to Tanu converts her own accumulated humiliation (hunger, unpaid labour, Auro's look of pity, “the kind one might reserve for a wretched dog” B4P9) into Tanu's exposure. The narration refuses to make this heroic: its motives arrive only as excuses, “Or at least that’s how you justify your words for days afterward” B4P21. In this story, self-assertion appears only as betrayal. The researcher's full-text reading reaches the same point: her final act is “both empowerment and betrayal”. The key-word label emotional manipulation and the AI's self-assertion, spite and moral ambiguity each catch only one side of it.

7.3 Intersections: what Fig. 5 shows and what it misses

Gender is the hub. Gender carries the most evidence (55 items) and overlaps most with emotional vulnerability (19) and sexuality (15). Read interpretively, gender is the channel through which class and sexuality become felt as vulnerability. The narrator is positioned at once by salary, marital status, sexual experience and family history. None of these alone explains her final question; together they do. This is the intersectional claim, used here in an extended sense. Crenshaw developed the concept to show how race and gender compound discrimination against Black women in law (Crenshaw, 1989, 1991). Applying it to class, sexuality and gender in a middle-class Delhi story borrows its structural insight (positions compound rather than add up) and not its original legal context.

7.5 Conclusions

  1. How does the story represent the intersections of gender, sexuality, class and emotional vulnerability? Through a single evening in which display and hunger, protection and possession, and the closet and its disclosure are bound together. Gender is the medium. Marriage is the institution that holds class security and sexual silence in place. Vulnerability, passed from mother to daughter and from the narrator to Tanu, is the force that breaks the structure open.
  2. How can synthetic reading reveal these structures? By making each reader's selections visible and checkable. The AI traced recurring social patterns across all four blocks; the human reader traced the narrator's inner life across blocks and judged the final act most precisely; comparing the full-text interpretation with the key words showed that apparent AI “discoveries” can be artefacts of how a human reading is summarised. A dataset of verified quotations made every claim traceable to the text, and the coding's blind spot (class and sexuality) showed where close reading had to take over. Synthetic reading did not produce the interpretation; it sharpened the questions that close reading then answered.

7.6 Limits of this interpretation and further work

  • One model and one run, and one human reader. Coding the human interpretation to themes is itself an analytical step; a second coder would test it.
  • The theoretical frames are largely Euro-American. The story is set in contemporary New Delhi; reading it alongside Indian scholarship on same-sex love (e.g. Vanita & Kidwai, 2000) and against the legal context after the Supreme Court read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code in 2018 (Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, 2018) would test whether these frames fit.
  • Next steps: run the same prompt with local models (Theme_JSON.py --source local-llm) several times to test stability; add a second human coder for the key-word mapping and the full-text coding; consider a theme tagged with both class and sexuality and see whether Fig. 5 changes.

Limitations and research ethics

Partial reading

The highlighted passages are the opening of each block (about 27% of the words). First impressions from openings can over-weight how a block begins and miss turns that come later, as Block 2 shows.

AI hallucination

Quotations are machine-verified against the corpus; interpretations are not and remain arguments. Verified quotations can still be placed under a theme that a different reader would reject.

Bias in LLM interpretation

Models trained mostly on English and Western sources may read gender, sexuality and class in an Indian setting through foreign frames. The narrator's sexuality is never stated in the text; the extraction treats it as shown (denial, fantasy, ambivalence), not declared.

Contextual limits

One short story read without author context, publication context or reception. Explicit/inferential labels describe the quotation, so 88 of 93 "explicit" does not mean the themes are uncontested.

Small corpus

About 4,300 words: counts describe this extraction of this story, not general patterns. Dimension tags are fixed per theme, so Fig. 5 partly reflects the coding scheme.

Copyright and responsible use

The story is a copyrighted work supplied by the researcher for study. If this repository is public, remove corpus/The_Party.txt (and the passages shown above) or obtain permission; keep only short quotations. Do not upload the story to third-party services without permission.

One-run, one-model

A single run of one model is not a replication. Running Theme_JSON.py --source local-llm with other models would test stability.
